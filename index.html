Do Knowledge- and Reasoning-Oriented Benchmarks Induce Stable Representation Signatures in LLMs?

Parsa Rahimi1,2, Azim Dehghani Amirabad3
1EPFL, 2Idiap, 3Independent Researcher
Read the paper ↗ arXiv ↗ Code ↗ Artifacts ↗

Activations decode a knowledge/reasoning benchmark label in five LLMs, but the prompt text alone does nearly as well, and the best layer does not transfer across task panels.

Abstract

Internal signatures could support monitoring, adaptive computation, and mechanistic analysis, but benchmark prompts can be separable without any shared computational distinction. We test whether a prespecified knowledge/reasoning label defines a reusable internal signature in five contemporary 7B to 31B models. We treat the task as the unit of inference: probes hold out complete tasks, labels are permuted across tasks, and the best layer and stream are reselected for every permutation.

Raw probes recover an eight-versus-eight-subject MMLU partition in all five models (macro-F1 0.78–0.81), but prompt text predicts it nearly as well: under an identical probe configuration, the activation advantage is only 0.017–0.070 macro-F1 and is not significant. At the prespecified 0.01 level, lexical adjustment removes significance while the adjusted states still identify individual subjects, and the selected site changes across task panels and selection rules. Additionally, two released base-training trajectories show no common direction. These pooled layerwise measures recover the task label but identify no transferable knowledge/reasoning signature at the tested sensitivity, which cannot exclude moderate effects. The resulting protocol gives future internal monitors and intervention studies a sequence of tests to pass.

The Study in a Nutshell

Question. Does a knowledge/reasoning benchmark label identify model processing beyond task and prompt identity?

Benchmark suites routinely split tasks into knowledge recall and multi-step reasoning. If a model processed the two groups differently, that internal difference could drive monitors, adaptive computation, or causal interventions. A probe that merely tells the tasks apart is not enough: it can latch onto topic, vocabulary, and format.

We study OLMo 3 7B, Apertus 1.5 8B, Qwen 3.5 9B and 27B, and Gemma 4 31B on 16 MMLU subjects (eight knowledge-oriented, eight reasoning-oriented; 100 questions each). Read-only hooks record the token-mixing update, the feed-forward update, and the residual state at every layer. Probes are always evaluated on held-out subjects, labels are permuted across whole subjects (all 12,870 balanced labelings), and the best layer and stream are reselected for every labeling.

Finding. Raw states predict the label in every model, but a probe on the prompt text, run through the same estimator, is nearly as good; the gain is significant in no model. The best layer moves between task panels and selectors, and two training trajectories move in opposite directions.

Use. The protocol — task-level holdout and permutation, a matched prompt comparison, transfer tests, and detection calibration — tests a representation signal before it guides monitoring, adaptive computation, or causal intervention.

The evidence

Four findings, one caution

Recovering a benchmark label does not establish a transferable knowledge/reasoning signature.

01 Activations decode the label, and prompt text nearly matches them

At the selected site, held-out-subject probes reach macro-F1 0.78–0.81 and are significant in all five models. A character n-gram probe on the prompt text, with the same folds, feature width, and classifier, reaches 0.755. The activation gain sits inside its exact permutation null everywhere.

02 Splitting by example inflates the evidence

On an arbitrary alphabetical split of the same 16 subjects, a probe whose training and test sets share subjects looks highly significant. Holding out whole subjects removes the effect: examples from one subject are not independent evidence.

03 The best layer moves between task panels

Within each model we select the layer and stream with the largest mean-shift ratio. Only OLMo and Apertus keep their site across the two MMLU panels, and no model keeps it across all three. A covariance-whitened selector picks a different site in every model, although its maxima stay significant.

04 Training trajectories point in opposite directions

Across seven OLMo 3 7B and eleven Apertus 1.0 8B base-training checkpoints, accuracy rises on both task groups, while the depth-averaged mean-shift ratio falls in OLMo and rises in Apertus. Neither path is monotonic, and each trajectory is a single training run.
